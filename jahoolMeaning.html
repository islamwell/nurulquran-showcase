One word. Spoken once in the whole Qur'ān.

Some Qur'ānic words appear hundreds of times. جَهُولًا appears exactly one time — in the very last portrait the Qur'ān paints of the human being carrying God's Trust.

Because it is said only once, every letter is loaded with profound insight. This study unpacks the word step by step: its three-letter root, its intensive grammatical pattern, its home verse, and its wider family across the Qur'ān — all in clear, accessible English.

The quickest possible answer

جَهُولًا means: "someone who is deeply and chronically unaware" — not just a person who happens not to know something in passing, but one whose nature is marked by not knowing, acting recklessly without forethought.

جَهِلَhe did not know / he acted foolishly — the basic verb
جَاهِلan ignorant person — standard description
جَهُولdeeply, chronically unknowing — the intensive form
Chapter One

The Root: ج · ه · ل

In Arabic, almost every word grows from a small root of three letters — like a seed. Change the shape around the seed and you unlock an entire family of related meanings. Tap each letter to explore.

جJīm

ج – ه – ل

j · h · l — read right to left. Together they carry one core idea: not-knowing, and behaving rashly because of it. Tap each letter to explore its role in the root.

01 عَدَمُ الْعِلْم

Not knowing

The plainest sense: the absence of knowledge. jahl is the exact opposite of ʿilm (knowledge). An empty vessel where understanding ought to reside.

02 السَّفَه وَالطَّيْش

Acting foolishly

Classical Arabic also uses jahl for hot-headed, reckless, and aggressive behavior — the exact opposite of ḥilm (dignified forbearance and self-restraint). Not merely an empty mind, but an untamed, hasty impulse.

03 الْجَاهِلِيَّة

A whole way of life

From the exact same root: Jāhiliyyah. In the Qur'ān, it names not a mere historical era, but an ongoing mindset — judging, thinking, behaving, and reacting as though divine revelation and higher guidance never arrived.

Chapter Two

Jahl has two opposites

This is the single most valuable linguistic key to understanding this root. Classical scholars note that jahl stands directly against two distinct virtues — which is why the simple English word "ignorance" never captures its full weight.

عِلْم ʿilm — Knowledge If jahl is the opposite of ʿilm, then the jāhil is someone whose mind is unlettered and empty. The remedy is learning and truth.
or
حِلْم ḥilm — Forbearance If jahl is the opposite of ḥilm, then the jāhil is someone whose temper is wild and impulsive — rash, defiant, unrestrained. The remedy is spiritual discipline.

Why this matters for جَهُولًا

In Surah Al-Aḥzāb 33:72, both dimensions apply at once, and classical commentators emphasize both. The human being who volunteers to carry God's Sacred Trust is described as someone who does not truly comprehend the immense weight of what he has undertaken (the lack of ʿilm) — and someone rash enough to seize it boldly without fear (the absence of ḥilm). The immense mountains, by contrast, had the awe and self-restraint to decline.

Scholarly perspective: Linguistic masters and classical lexicons note both aspects — Goldziher highlighted the ḥilm contrast; Franz Rosenthal underscored the ʿilm contrast; classical Arabic lexicons like Lisān al-ʿArab confirm that both virtues stand as mutual counters to jahl.

Chapter Three

The Pattern: فَعُول — the intensive mould

Arabic does not need qualifiers like "very" or "exceedingly." It reshapes the architecture of the word itself. When you place the root letters ج-ه-ل into the morphological pattern فَعُول, you obtain جَهُول — an intensive active noun (ṣīghat al-mubālaghah): denoting one who is deeply, chronically, and characteristically unaware.

The same pattern in words you already recognize

Here lies a sublime linguistic symmetry: فَعُول is the exact mould the Qur'ān uses both for God's overflowing divine attributes and for humanity's chronic flaws.

ظَلُومẓalūmits paired twin in 33:72 — deeply unjust, committing immense wrong against himself

✦ Notice: Ghafūr and jahūl share the identical pattern. God is boundless in His forgiveness; humanity is chronic in its unawareness. The very grammar mirrors the theological relationship.

Why the final ـًا (tanwīn)?

The marks on the final letter are tanwīn fatḥ. They serve two distinct grammatical functions:

  • 1. Indefiniteness — it renders the noun indefinite: designating a creature characteristically unaware, establishing a qualitative universal portrait.
  • 2. Accusative case (manṣūb) — because it serves as the grammatical predicate of كَانَ (khabar kāna): "indeed he was and has always been… jahūlan."

Why كَانَ matters

English translations often render this as "he was ignorant," which might sound like a past, finished episode. In Qur'ānic Arabic, when كَانَ pairs with intensive adjectives, it signals an enduring, perpetual human condition:

"He has ever been, and remains by default, profoundly unjust and profoundly unaware."

This framing explains why the verse serves as a universal diagnosis of humanity's raw state — not an indictment of a single isolated individual.

Chapter Four

Its one and only home: Sūrah Al-Aḥzāb 33:72

Tap any word to inspect its meaning and grammar. The two golden words at the conclusion are the intensive twin adjectives.

جَهُولًا
utterly, habitually ignorant Root ج-ه-ل on the intensive pattern فَعُول. Appears only here in the entire Qur'ān. ← tap other words

"Indeed, We offered the Trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, and they declined to bear it and feared it; but man undertook to bear it. Indeed, he was unjust and ignorant."

Ṣaḥīḥ International · Al-Aḥzāb 33:72

A cosmic offer, and one volunteer

Picture the vastness of the scene: the heavens, the earth, and the mountains — the largest and most enduring creations — are presented with the Trust. They decline. Not in disobedience, but out of أَشْفَقْنَ — a trembling, reverent awe born from understanding just how weighty the responsibility is.

Then the human being steps forward and carries it. God comments immediately: إِنَّهُ كَانَ ظَلُومًا جَهُولًا.

The cosmic creations understood the weight and trembled. Humanity failed to comprehend the weight, yet boldly volunteered. That is the reality of جَهُولًا.

الأَمَانَة — al-Amānah, "the Trust"

Classical commentators provide multifaceted insight into this Trust. Abū Ḥayyān (al-Baḥr al-Muḥīṭ) notes that the encompassing meaning is everything with which humanity is entrusted — every command, every moral prohibition, and every obligation of worship and life; and Tafsīr Maẓharī explains that the moral accountability of the Sharīʿah constitutes the core of the amānah.

Moral Agency & Choice
the awe-inspiring capacity of conscious spiritual choice
Accountability
the duty of answering directly for one's actions before God
Stewardship
caring for the earth, bodies, resources, and fellow creatures
Fidelity to Faith
guarding what is entrusted to the human heart

Ibn Kathīr relates the prophetic reminder that the Trust is deeply anchored in the depths of human nature, urging believers to guard it against neglect.

Not a curse — a diagnosis

Read superficially, the words ظَلُومًا جَهُولًا might sound harsh. Understood within its full Qur'ānic context, it is a truthful diagnostic accompanying an extraordinary honor.

  • It reveals the inherent stakes. Only a creature capable of catastrophic failing can achieve genuine moral victory. The mountains cannot disobey, so they cannot consciously choose faith.
  • It directly prepares verse 73. Ayah 33:73 immediately follows: God accepts the repentance of believing men and women, turning toward them in forgiveness. The vulnerability diagnosed in 72 is met by divine grace in 73.
  • It culminates the Sūrah. Sūrah Al-Aḥzāb reaches its pinnacle with humanity's chronic frailty answered by God's boundless attributes: Ghafūran Raḥīmā (Ever-Forgiving, Most Merciful).

…he was unjust and ignorant.

Many conventional English versions simplify جَهُولًا to a flat "ignorant," missing the intensive power. To feel the true depth in English, understand it as: "ever so deeply and chronically unknowing."

Chapter Six

How the scholars read it

The lexical tradition

Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad's Kitāb al-ʿAyn — the earliest surviving Arabic dictionary — already frames the root by its opposition to ʿilm. Later lexicographers (Ibn Qutayba, al-Azharī, and eventually Ibn Manẓūr in Lisān al-ʿArab) highlight the moral dimension: impulsiveness, haste, arrogance, and the unrestrained assertion of ego.

The tafsīr tradition

On 33:72, Ibn Kathīr explains the paired descriptions ظَلُومًا جَهُولًا as shedding light on why human beings so frequently stumble under the Trust; al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī gather early transmissions regarding the solemn cosmic offer; while Abū Ḥayyān takes al-amānah in its most comprehensive sense — all sacred duties and trusts.

The modern debate

Reading pre-Islamic poetry where the root appears frequently, scholars observed that jahl often denoted passionate rashness and defiant pride — the polar counter to ḥilm. Other scholars emphasized its cognitive lack — the antonym of ʿilm. Modern linguistic consensus recognizes that both dimensions are inextricably woven into the root.

A ḥadīth worth knowing

The Prophet ﷺ once cautioned Abū Dharr during an intense disagreement: “You are a person who still has a trace of jāhiliyyah in you.” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 30). The enduring wisdom is clear: jahl is not merely an extinct historical period; it is a recurring human inclination of rashness and arrogance that every soul must vigilantly refine.

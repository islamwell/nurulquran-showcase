One word. Spoken once in the whole Qur'ān.

Some Qur'ānic words appear hundreds of times. جَهُولًا appears exactly one time — in the very last portrait the Qur'ān paints of the human being carrying God's Trust.

Because it is said only once, every letter is loaded. This app takes the word apart slowly: its three-letter root, its intensive shape, its home verse, and its family across the Qur'ān — all in plain English.

The quickest possible answer

جَهُولًا (jahūlan) means: "someone who is very ignorant" — not just a person who happens not to know something, but a person whose habit is not knowing, and who acts recklessly because of it.

جَهِلَhe did not know / he acted foolishly — the verb
جَاهِلan ignorant person — plain description
جَهُولdeeply, chronically ignorant — the intense form
Chapter One

The Root: ج · ه · ل

In Arabic, almost every word grows from a small root of usually three letters — like a seed. Change the shape around the seed and you get a whole garden of related words. Tap each letter.

جJīm
هHāʾ
لLām
ج – ه – ل

j · h · l — read right to left. Together they carry one core idea: not-knowing, and behaving badly because of it. Tap a letter to hear how it is pronounced.

01 عَدَمُ الْعِلْم

Not knowing

The plainest sense: the absence of knowledge. jahl is the exact opposite of ʿilm (knowledge). An empty cup where understanding should be.

02 السَّفَه وَالطَّيْش

Acting foolishly

Older Arabic (and pre-Islamic poetry) used jahl for hot-headed, reckless, arrogant behaviour — the opposite of ḥilm (calm self-restraint). Not an empty head, but an untamed temper.

03 الْجَاهِلِيَّة

A whole way of life

From the same root: Jāhiliyyah. In the Qur'ān it names not a date on a calendar but a mindset — judging, thinking, dressing and raging as though revelation never arrived.

Chapter Two

Jahl has two opposites

This is the single most useful thing to know about this root. Scholars have long noticed that jahl stands against two different virtues — which is why English "ignorance" never quite captures it.

عِلْم ʿilm — Knowledge If jahl is the opposite of ʿilm, then the jāhil is someone whose mind is empty. The cure is learning.
or
حِلْم ḥilm — Forbearance If jahl is the opposite of ḥilm, then the jāhil is someone whose temper is untamed — rash, arrogant, violent. The cure is character.

Why this matters for جَهُولًا

In 33:72 both senses fit at once, and most commentators let both stand. The human being who picks up God's Trust is described as someone who does not truly grasp what he has taken on (against ʿilm) — and someone rash enough to grab it anyway (against ḥilm). The mountains, by contrast, had the ḥilm to tremble.

Note: Orientalists Goldziher and Izutsu emphasised the ḥilm reading; Franz Rosenthal argued for the ʿilm reading; Peter Webb accepts that both are true in different contexts. Classical Arabic lexicons carry both.

Chapter Three

The Pattern: فَعُول — the "extra-much" shape

Arabic doesn't need the word "very." It reshapes the word itself. Pour the root ج-ه-ل into the mould فَعُول (faʿūl) and you get جَهُول — an intensive (ṣīghat al-mubālaghah): not "ignorant" but "ignorant through and through."

The same mould, other words you already know

Here is the beautiful symmetry: فَعُول is the mould the Qur'ān uses both for God's overflowing mercy and for the human being's overflowing flaws.

WordReads asMeaning — with the "extra-much"
جَهُولjahūlour word — profoundly, habitually unknowing
ظَلُومẓalūmits twin in the same verse — deeply unjust, a great wrongdoer
غَفُورGhafūra Name of Allah — abundantly forgiving, again and again
شَكُورShakūra Name of Allah — immensely appreciative of the smallest good
وَدُودWadūda Name of Allah — overflowing in love and affection
صَبُورṣabūrenormously patient — patience as a permanent trait
كَفُورkafūrdeeply ungrateful (Qur'ān 17:27, of Satan)

✦ Notice: Ghafūr and jahūl are built identically. God is intensely forgiving; we are intensely unaware. The grammar itself tells the story of the relationship.

Why the final ـًا (an)?

The two little marks on the end are tanwīn fatḥ. They do two jobs:

  • 1. "a / an" — the word is indefinite: a deeply ignorant one, not the.
  • 2. Accusative case (manṣūb) — because it is the predicate of كَانَ (khabar kāna): "he was / has ever been … jahūlan."

Why كَانَ (kāna) matters

English translators write "he was ignorant," which sounds like a one-time event in the past. But here كَانَ plus an intensive noun signals a standing, timeless condition — closer to:

"He has always been, and remains, one utterly unjust and utterly unaware."

This is why the verse reads as a description of humanity as such — not an accusation of one person.

Chapter Four

Its one and only home: Sūrah Al-Aḥzāb 33:72

Tap any word to open it up. The two golden words at the end are the intensives.

جَهُولًا
jahūlan utterly, habitually ignorant Root ج-ه-ل on the intensive pattern فَعُول. Appears only here in the entire Qur'ān. ← tap other words

"Indeed, We offered the Trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, and they declined to bear it and feared it; but man undertook to bear it. Indeed, he was unjust and ignorant."

Ṣaḥīḥ International · Al-Aḥzāb 33:72

A cosmic offer, and one volunteer

Picture it: the heavens, the earth, and the mountains — the largest, oldest, strongest things we can name — are shown the Trust. They refuse. Not out of rebellion, but out of أَشْفَقْنَ (ashfaqna): a trembling awe, a fear born of understanding exactly how heavy the thing is.

Then the human being steps forward and takes it. And God comments, immediately: إِنَّهُ كَانَ ظَلُومًا جَهُولًا.

The mountains understood and declined. The human did not fully understand — and accepted. That is jahūlan.

الأَمَانَة — al-Amānah, "the Trust"

Commentators offer overlapping answers, and the majority view is broad. Abū Ḥayyān (al-Baḥr al-Muḥīṭ) reports that the apparent meaning is everything one is entrusted with — every command, every prohibition, every matter of religion and worldly life; and Tafsīr Maẓharī states that the whole body of the Sharīʿah's obligations and prohibitions is the amānah.

Free will
the terrifying gift of genuine moral choice
Accountability
the duties of revelation, and answering for them
Stewardship
caretaking the earth, bodies, wealth, other people
Fidelity
keeping what is deposited with you — literally and morally

Ibn Kathīr cites the ḥadīth that amānah will be lifted from hearts until "people trade but hardly anyone fulfils a trust," leaving only a trace "like a blister from a rolled coal."

Not a curse — a diagnosis

Read coldly, ẓalūman jahūlan sounds like contempt. Read in context, it is closer to a medical note attached to an extraordinary honour.

  • It explains the risk. Only a creature who could fail this badly could have anything meaningful to succeed at. The mountains cannot betray a trust; therefore they cannot keep one either.
  • It sets up the very next verse. 33:73 immediately continues: God will punish the hypocrites and idolaters, and turn in mercy to the believing men and women. The flaw named in 72 is the flaw mercy answers in 73.
  • It is the last word of the sūrah. Al-Aḥzāb ends on humanity's weakness and God's forgiveness held in the same breath.

…he was unjust and ignorant.

Ṣaḥīḥ International

…they are truly wrongful to themselves and ignorant of the consequences.

Dr Mustafa Khattab · The Clear Qur'ān

Surely he is wrong-doing, ignorant.

Abul Aʿlā Mawdūdī · Tafhīm al-Qur'ān

…car il est très injuste envers lui-même et très ignorant.

French (Hamidullah) — note how French keeps the intensive with "très"

بے شک وہ بڑا ظالم اور جاہل ہے

Urdu (Mawdūdī) — "بڑا / baṛā" = great, carrying the intensity

Most English versions flatten jahūlan to plain "ignorant" and lose the intensive. French and Urdu keep it. If you want the full flavour in English, read: "ever so deeply unknowing."

Chapter Five

The Word Family across the Qur'ān

The root ج-ه-ل and its derivatives occur roughly 24 times in the Qur'ān, spread over about 17 sūrahs (as counted in al-Muʿjam al-Mufahras). Here are the shapes it takes.

جَهِلَjahila
verb · "he did not know / acted foolishly"
جَاهِلjāhil
active participle · "an ignorant one" (pl. جَاهِلُون / جَاهِلِين)
جَهَالَةjahālah
verbal noun · "a state of ignorance"
جَاهِلِيَّةjāhiliyyah
abstract noun · "ignorance as a way of life" — 4×
جَهُولjahūl
intensive · our word — 1× only

Chapter Six

How the scholars read it

The lexical tradition

Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad's Kitāb al-ʿAyn — the earliest surviving Arabic dictionary — already frames the root by its opposition to ʿilm. Later lexicographers (Ibn Qutayba, al-Azharī, and eventually Ibn Manẓūr in Lisān al-ʿArab) add the moral colouring: recklessness, arrogance, rule of the strong over the weak.

The tafsīr tradition

On 33:72, Ibn Kathīr reads the pair ẓalūman jahūlan as explaining why humanity so often fails the Trust; al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī gather the reports on the offer to the heavens and mountains; Abū Ḥayyān takes amānah in its widest sense — "all that one is entrusted with."

The modern debate

Ignaz Goldziher and Toshihiko Izutsu, reading pre-Islamic poetry (Imruʾ al-Qays uses the root eight times; al-Nābighah six), argued jahl there means barbarism — the antonym of ḥilm. Franz Rosenthal argued it means ignorance — the antonym of ʿilm. Peter Webb accepts both, depending on context.

A ḥadīth worth knowing

The Prophet ﷺ once cautioned Abū Dharr (after an argument in which he spoke harshly of a companion’s mother): “You are a person who still has a trace of jāhiliyyah in you.” When Abū Dharr asked if this could still be true despite his faith and age, the Prophet ﷺ reminded him of human dignity and brotherhood (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 30). The lesson is timeless: jahl is not just an ancient historical era safely behind us; it is a human impulse that every believer must actively guard against.
